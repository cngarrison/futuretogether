# The Coding Depresh: Craft-Identity Displacement as a Distinct AI Transition Story

## Core Argument

The dominant public narrative about AI and developers is a labour-market story: job displacement, hiring slowdowns, junior developer pipelines drying up. That story is real. But there is a different displacement happening simultaneously — one that hits experienced practitioners who are, by outward measures, doing fine. They are shipping more, faster. They feel worse anyway.

Matsuoka calls it the "Coding Depresh" (after comedian Gary Gulman's diminutive for his own clinical depression — a smaller word that made the thing sayable). The mechanism is identity disconfirmation, not economic threat. For developers whose answer to "who are you?" was inseparable from "I am the person who writes excellent code," AI tools arrive at the identity layer first. The job isn't threatened. The self is.

The cleanest evidence: George Violaris, a 15-year veteran, ships a data pipeline with AI assistance in an afternoon — work that would have taken three days. He expected pride. He got grief. "Not for a person. For an identity."

This is not unique to software. It is the shape of every craft-automation transition. Matsuoka's historical parallel is exact and worth dwelling on.

## The Canut Analogy — and Why It Matters for FT

In the early 19th century, the *canuts* were the silk weavers of Lyon, clustered in the Croix-Rousse district. Master-craft work, identity-defining — the skill lived in the fingers. Then Joseph Marie Jacquard demonstrated a loom that wove complex patterns automatically, driven by punched cards, doing in a single pass what had required a weaver and an assistant by hand.

The displacement split along a single axis: the canut whose sense of self lived in the act of weaving — the tight and skilled handwork itself — was displaced at the identity layer. The canut whose relationship was with the silk itself, the finished cloth, still had somewhere to stand. The fabric still got made. More of it, in fact.

Jacquard's punched cards are a direct ancestor of the computer — they ran into Babbage's Analytical Engine and later Hollerith's tabulators and the IBM punch card. The mechanism that displaced the weaver became the machine the coder works on, now automating a layer of the coder's craft. Same lineage, one more turn.

This is the FT frame: **we are living through one more iteration of a transition pattern that has repeated across industrial history.** The 1891 parallel applies. We are in the window where the displacement is visible but the frameworks for responding haven't solidified.

## The Fault Line

The fault line is not seniority, skill, or domain. It is the answer you would have given, before any of this started, to one question: who are you as a developer?

If the answer was inseparable from authorship — the line-by-line craft, the mechanical understanding of every decision — AI arrives at the identity layer. The grief is the correct, proportional response. It scales with how much of yourself you invested.

If the answer was closer to "I build the thing that exists at the end," the same tools feel like a gain. You were always pointed at the cloth, not the weave.

The Anthropic engineer's version — quoted in Anthropic's own internal research on AI-assisted work — is the skeleton key:

> "I thought that I really enjoyed writing code, and I think instead I actually just enjoy what I *get* out of writing code."

AI unbundles what was always bundled before. You couldn't get the output without the authorship. Now you can. The question becomes: which half did you actually love?

## Key Quotable Framings

- **"The ego death was real. 'I am a person who writes excellent code' had to die."** — George Violaris, developer, 15 years' experience, March 2026
- **"I thought that I really enjoyed writing code, and I think instead I actually just enjoy what I get out of writing code."** — Anthropic engineer, quoted in internal AI-assisted work research
- **"The depresh is in remish."** — Gary Gulman (the original, applied here as register: a thing managed rather than defeated)
- **Canut framing**: the canut whose love was the weaving vs the canut whose love was the cloth. Both were skilled. The automation found only one of them at the identity layer.
- **Stack Overflow 2025**: usage climbed to 84%; favorable sentiment fell to 60%; distrust outran trust (46% vs 33%); 66% cite "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite" as top frustration. People are adopting tools they feel worse about.
- **METR study retired**: the most-cited evidence that AI slows experienced developers (19% slower) has been formally retired by the same researchers. Their redesigned measurement shows ~18% speedup. The measurement broke because 30–50% of developers refused to submit tasks they expected AI to speed up — they wouldn't give up the tools. The stopwatch reversed.

## Implications for Community Preparedness

**1. Craft-identity displacement is not the same as labour displacement.** Communities and organisations that treat all AI anxiety as job-loss fear will miss this entirely. The developers most affected by the Depresh are frequently the most experienced and most skilled — the ones who invested most in the craft. Their distress is not irrationality or resistance to change. It is proportional grief.

**2. Pushing adoption before making room for grief produces adoption without standards.** Matsuoka's advice to CTOs is directly applicable to any community leader or organisation facilitating AI transitions: "Don't push adoption before you've made room for the grief. The engineers who feel the Depresh most sharply are frequently your best ones, who invested most in the craft. Their standards are the feature, not the bug. Burn that instinct down to force faster adoption and you'll get the adoption and lose the standards."

**3. The 66% "almost right" problem is a collective burden.** The exhaustion of vigilance — working with something you don't trust, that demands verification you can't delegate — is not sustainable at the individual level. Communities need structures for sharing the verification burden. This is a new form of collective infrastructure.

**4. The craft instinct is not the enemy.** The taste, architectural judgment, and accumulated pattern-recognition that took years to build are precisely what makes AI tools usable rather than dangerous. The path is not to demolish the craft identity but to reframe it: from author of code to author of outcomes. The instinct that fires when something is "almost right but not quite" is the sharpest instrument the experienced practitioner owns.

**5. The Canut window.** We are in the same position relative to AI as early-19th-century Lyon was to the Jacquard loom — after enough displacement is visible, before the frameworks for responding have solidified. The question communities can ask now, while it is still early enough to matter: which of our collective identities are rooted in the mechanics of the work, and which are rooted in what the work produces? The answer shapes what can survive the transition.
