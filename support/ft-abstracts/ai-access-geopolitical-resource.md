# Who Gets the Most Powerful AI? Access as Geopolitical Resource

## Core Argument

Frontier AI access is becoming a controlled geopolitical resource, with the U.S. government now acting as a gatekeeper. In the same week, both OpenAI (GPT-5.6 Sol family) and Anthropic (Mythos 5, Fable 5) confirmed their most capable models are restricted to ~20–100 vetted U.S. partners by government request. Austria's response — proposing to host Anthropic on European soil — is the first concrete example of a government-level attempt to route around this access asymmetry.

The deeper argument: AI access is not neutral or universal. It is being structured, from the beginning, with political access controls that will shape who can participate in the AI economy. Communities that don't notice this are not making a neutral choice — they are accepting someone else's.

This is not a future concern. It is the present arrangement.

## Key Quotable Framings

- "The most capable AI tools are not available to most of the world — by deliberate design, not technical limitation."
- "Austria is trying to buy a seat at the table. Most communities don't even know the table exists."
- "In 1891, access to the telegraph network determined who could participate in the emerging industrial economy. The question of who controls that access was settled before most communities understood it was being asked."
- "Frontier AI is the new critical infrastructure — and it is being built with access controls that look a lot like export controls."
- "The gap isn't between people who use AI and people who don't. It's between those who have access to the most capable tier and those making do with what's permitted."

## Implications for Community Preparedness

1. **Access inequality is real and widening now.** The gap between frontier models (gated to vetted U.S. partners) and what most organisations can access is not shrinking — it's being institutionalised. Communities and organisations outside this tier face a capability disadvantage that is political, not just economic.

2. **Awareness is the first step.** Most communities — including technically sophisticated ones — don't know that the most capable AI models are not available to them. Understanding that "AI" is a stratified resource, not a uniform one, is itself a preparedness outcome.

3. **Diversification strategies matter.** Provider-agnostic approaches, open-weight model deployment, and regional AI infrastructure (like the Austrian proposal) are not just technical decisions — they are community resilience decisions. Communities that depend on a single provider's frontier tier have an unmodelled access dependency.

4. **The governance window is now.** The frameworks that will govern frontier AI access — who gets it, on what terms, under what conditions — are being written now. Waiting until the frameworks have solidified, as with industrial capitalism after 1891, means accepting the terms set by others.

5. **Data: the access asymmetry is stark.** GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI's most capable model) is available to approximately 20 organisations globally. Mythos 5 (Anthropic's most capable) to approximately 100 vetted U.S. organisations. The rest of the world uses previous tiers.

## Suggested Article Structure

1. **Opening scene:** Austria making a formal proposal to host an American AI lab on European soil — the act itself tells you something has changed.

2. **The week it became a pattern:** Both OpenAI and Anthropic gating frontier models in the same week. Name the models; name the numbers (20 partners, 100 partners). This is not theoretical.

3. **Why this matters more than it looks:** The capability gap between gated frontier models and what's broadly available is not small. These are the models being used for national security, scientific research, and strategic decision-making. Being outside the gate is not neutral.

4. **The 1891 parallel:** Industrial infrastructure access shaped economic participation for generations. The frameworks were set before most communities understood the question was being asked. Where are we in that timeline right now?

5. **What communities can actually do:**
   - Understand the tier they're on (most don't)
   - Support regional AI infrastructure initiatives
   - Advocate for public access provisions in AI governance frameworks
   - Build with provider-agnostic approaches where possible
   - Participate in the governance conversation now, not after the frameworks solidify

6. **Closing:** The question is not whether AI access will be unequal. It already is. The question is whether communities will shape the terms of that inequality before or after they're set.
