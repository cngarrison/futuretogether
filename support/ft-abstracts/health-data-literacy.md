## Core argument

A Nobel laureate running Google DeepMind has said — publicly, and with growing confidence — that all disease could be cured within 10–20 years. His revised position: the timeline hasn't shifted, but it has "hardened." That's not a speculative claim anymore; it's a credentialled, public commitment entering mainstream discourse.

The implication most people miss: medical superintelligence will only be as good as the data you feed it. The people who understand this transition and start collecting biomarker data, fitness tracking, blood panels, and continuous health monitoring *now* will have a structural advantage when the tools arrive. The people who don't — or who can't — won't.

This is not primarily a story about technology. It's a story about a new kind of preparedness gap opening right now, quietly, while most communities are focused on other things. The window to act is open. It won't stay open.

## Key quotable framings

- **Demis Hassabis (Nobel laureate, CEO Google DeepMind)**: "They've not shifted, but they've hardened, they've tightened. I'm very confident." — on his timeline for AI curing all diseases within a human lifetime.
- **Rowan Cheung (The Rundown AI)**: "Drag-and-dropping 5–10 years of health data will be literally life-changing for folks disciplined enough to start collecting today."
- **The gap these quotes reveal**: both assume the speaker already knows to do this. Neither asks who *doesn't* know — and what that will cost them.

## Implications for community preparedness

**This is an information asymmetry problem, not a technology problem.** The tools for collecting health data (fitness trackers, blood panels, continuous glucose monitors, sleep trackers) already exist and are increasingly affordable. The barrier is not cost — it's knowledge. Specifically: knowing that this data will matter, understanding which data is worth collecting, and having the habit infrastructure to collect it consistently.

Communities that are already AI-aware will be disproportionately represented among people who start collecting now. Communities that aren't — which often correlates with communities that are already health-disadvantaged — will arrive at the medical AI transition with less data, and therefore get less from it. This compounds existing health inequality rather than resolving it.

**The 1891 parallel**: we are in the same position relative to medical AI as 1891 was to industrial capitalism. The transition is visible and credentialled. The frameworks haven't solidified. The damage (in this case: a new form of health data inequality) is preventable if communities understand what's coming and act. After the transition is complete, the gap will be structural and very hard to close.

**What communities can do now** (concrete, not aspirational):
- Understand which data is worth collecting (not all wearables are equal; blood biomarkers are high-value)
- Build accessible, low-cost pathways to regular blood panel testing
- Advocate for health data portability so individuals own and control their records
- Recognise health data as a common/public good question — the same framing FT applies to other data domains
