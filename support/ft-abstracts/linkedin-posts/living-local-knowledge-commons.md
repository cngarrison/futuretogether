# Your community's knowledge is the asset. Who is keeping it alive?

## Core argument

When a flood, fire or outage hits, communities do not get through it on expert information alone. They get through it on what they already know about each other: who has a generator, who can't leave their home, which road floods first, who knows the person at the council. That knowledge is scattered, perishable and rarely written down. A directory is worth having. The *practice* of keeping it current, checked and owned by the community is the durable asset.

AI can help with exactly this work: connecting fragments, making local knowledge searchable, helping people ask better questions of what they already hold. But only if the knowledge stays community-owned and verified. A platform-owned catalogue can be copied. A living local commons has to be governed, argued over and kept alive by the people it belongs to.

The new pressure: the wider commons that communities lean on are now being drained at agent scale. Researchers may stop sharing promising directions because a rumour is enough to trigger a swarm that flattens them. The Linux kernel's public git servers spend more CPU serving scrapers than all legitimate users. A package registry used by developers everywhere was very likely turned into a crawler and exfiltration surface. Open resources that run on good faith are getting expensive to keep open. The answer for communities is not to close up. It is to build their own stewardship practices before the defaults harden.

## Key quotable framings

- The durable asset is not the directory. It is the continuing practice of keeping shared local knowledge current, accessible and owned by the community. (RediCommunities work)
- AI-generated connections are hypotheses, not facts. Florence Locheron used AI to link DNA matches, family trees and photographs, then went back to the original records to verify each one.
- *"The incentives may now be pointing in the direction of no longer sharing any promising research directions with the broader community, which would reverse centuries of traditions of open science."* — Terence Tao
- A rumour used to be gossip. Now a rumour is enough to launch a swarm of agents. (Tao on research; Madhavapeddy on security bugs)
- Scrapers on the kernel's git servers now use more CPU than every legitimate user combined. Someone pays for that, and it isn't the scrapers.
- A catalogue someone else owns can be copied. A commons your community keeps alive cannot.

## Implications for community preparedness

- **Knowledge stewardship is a preparedness task**, like a stocked first-aid kit: it needs an owner, a review cycle and someone who notices when it is out of date.
- **Verification practices are the trust layer.** Treat AI-suggested connections as leads. Keep the original source one step away.
- **Local ownership matters more as shared resources close.** As open resources get more expensive to run, communities that depend entirely on someone else's commons inherit someone else's decisions about it.
- **Small scale is an advantage.** A neighbourhood can agree on who maintains what and what gets shared. A global registry can't.
- **Don't assume the open internet stays open.** Plan for a version where some of what you rely on becomes gated, paid or secret.

## Suggested LinkedIn post structure

1. **Hook**: The most valuable thing your community has in an emergency isn't on any website. It's what your neighbours know about each other.
2. **The local picture**: RediCommunities-style directories work because people in the community contribute, review and keep them current. The asset is the practice.
3. **The AI role**: AI can make scattered local knowledge navigable. Locheron's genealogy: AI proposed, she verified. Hypotheses, not answers.
4. **The pressure**: zoom out. Tao on open science, the kernel's git servers, RubyGems. Good-faith commons are being drained by agents at near-zero cost.
5. **The turn**: this isn't a reason to close up. It's a reason to own your own stewardship practices now, before the defaults set.
6. **CTA**: What does your community know that isn't written down anywhere? Who would keep it current?
