# Who decides who gets the most powerful AI?

## Core argument

The U.S. government has established a new precedent: frontier AI models at a capability threshold it deems significant now require government sign-off before wider release. GPT-5.6 — considered to have the same capability level as the "Mythos" threshold — will be previewed only to government-approved partners, approved "customer by customer." The framing is security. The structural reality is: a small group of officials now decides which organisations get early access to transformative capability.

This is a new form of power asymmetry — not labour displacement, not market gatekeeping by AI agents, but *capability access gatekeeping*. The question communities should be asking is not "is this model safe?" but "who is in the room when access is granted, and by what criteria?"

## Key quotable framings

- *"As models approach Mythos-level, government sign-off may become a new step before wider release."* — The Rundown AI
- The framing is security; the precedent is gatekeeping.
- Approved "customer by customer" — access to transformative tools is now a permission, not a product.
- The 1891 parallel: new intermediaries and gatekeepers solidify before the frameworks to respond have formed.
- Who gets the most capable AI first shapes who gets the advantage longest.

## Implications for community preparedness

- **A new axis of AI inequality**: access to frontier capability is now formally tiered by government approval — not price, not skill, not need
- **The criteria are invisible**: what makes an organisation approved? How are communities, civil society, educators, or small businesses represented in that decision?
- **The window argument**: the frameworks for equitable AI access are not yet formed — this is the moment to ask the question, before the pattern hardens
- **FT's role**: helping communities see that AI governance is not just about safety rules, but about who holds the keys to capability — and whether that's accountable to anyone
