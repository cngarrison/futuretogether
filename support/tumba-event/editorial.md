**PREPARING FOR WHAT'S COMING — TOGETHER**

*Charlie Garrison, Tumbarumba*

---

Remember when fuel was over $2.50 a litre? It came back down — this time. But for a few weeks, the cost of getting to Wagga, running a farm vehicle, or just doing the weekly shop felt different. It was a reminder that the systems our region depends on aren't as reliable as we assume.

That vulnerability isn't going away. If anything, it's growing — and from more directions than most people are watching.

I've worked in technology for thirty years, the last three spent working daily with artificial intelligence. What I've been witnessing has fundamentally changed how I think about the next decade. The technology reshaping how people work and earn a living is advancing faster than public awareness — and that gap matters, because it's the gap between being prepared and being caught off-guard.

For those who haven't looked closely: two ideas are worth knowing exist. The first is the **alignment problem** — ensuring AI systems do what we actually intend, not just what we literally instruct. It sounds simple; it's one of the hardest unsolved problems in computer science. The second is the **singularity** — a threshold where AI becomes capable of improving itself beyond human oversight. Serious researchers believe we're approaching it. What's on the other side is binary: the best or worst outcome in human history. There's no middle ground, and no second attempt.

Around the middle of last year, something shifted for me. The more I understood what I was seeing in AI development, the harder it became to stay quiet. I started hosting informal coffee chats — just a table and whoever wanted to talk. Those conversations led to founding Future Together, an organisation built around community awareness and honest dialogue about what's coming. That commitment is what brings this conversation to Tumbarumba on 11 June — a free community talk, open to everyone, at Café Nest Cinema.

But the point of this piece isn't the event. It's the underlying question: how do we make our community more resilient, whatever comes?

The answer starts with things Tumbarumba already understands. The bushfires showed us that what carries people through a crisis isn't individual readiness — it's existing trust and existing relationships. The café owner who made free lunches for three weeks knew his neighbours. That foundation is irreplaceable, and it has to be built before it's needed.

In a remote community, food security is particularly real. We are a long way from regular supply chains, and those chains — as COVID showed, and fuel prices remind us — can be disrupted. Build relationships with local growers. Know where your food actually comes from. Support local businesses not just as commerce, but as community infrastructure. These aren't doomsday preparations — they're the kind of sensible resilience this district already has a tradition of.

And talk. With family, friends, anyone you care about. Not to alarm them — but because awareness is how preparation begins. The communities that navigate difficulty best are the ones that saw it coming together.
